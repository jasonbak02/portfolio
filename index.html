  4. 2025

    Havasupai Water Tower Rehabilitation

    Structural rehabilitation of a 50,000-gallon elevated water storage tank on the Havasupai Reservation, the potable water supply for more than 300 residents of the Havasupai Tribe. U.S. Public Health Service mechanical engineering internship, Parker, Arizona.

    ANSYS · FEA · Structural

    If the analysis was wrong, a community lost reliable access to clean water.

    Over allowable stress at the weld joints
    35%
    Lower maximum stress after rehabilitation
    42%
    Years of added service life
    ~25

    What I did

    • Ran finite element analysis of the tank structure in ANSYS.
    • Found stress concentrations at the weld joints about 35% over allowable limits, which pointed to the specific failure mode behind the water loss.
    • Modeled and validated a rehabilitation design that cut maximum stress by 42% and extended service life by roughly 25 years.
    • Because the failure was pinned down precisely, the fix could be a targeted rehabilitation instead of a full tank replacement.

    Design files and drawings are U.S. government property and aren't shown here.

About

I design and build mechanical systems in service of public health.

As a Mechanical Engineering Intern with the Indian Health Service through the U.S. Public Health Service, I support water, sanitation and health care infrastructure for tribal communities in the Southwest and Wisconsin. My work combines fieldwork and design. In the field, I’ve collected pressure logs, run flow tests, and done structural inspections at more than 20 sites, and back in the office I’ve used that data to design and model over 15 infrastructure systems in AutoCAD and Civil 3D. Those design packages helped secure $500,000 in federal funding, so getting the details right mattered. One of my main projects was the rehabilitation of a 50,000-gallon water tower on the Havasupai Reservation, where I used ANSYS to find overstressed weld joints and helped develop a targeted repair that extended the tank’s service life by about 25 years.

This summer I also worked on the $21 million expansion of the Sokaogon Chippewa Community’s health clinic in Mole Lake, Wisconsin, which triples the clinic to more than 30,000 square feet. I reviewed contractor bid proposals and interviewed candidates for the construction contract, planned emergency routes and the placement of exit signs, evacuation maps and fire extinguishers, and laid out furniture across the new space. What I value most about this work is how direct the impact is. The systems I help design decide whether families have reliable access to clean water and health care.
